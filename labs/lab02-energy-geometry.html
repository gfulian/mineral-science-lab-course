Laboratory 2 — From structure to energy

In the first laboratory a CIF was enough to describe where the atoms are and how symmetry generates a periodic crystal. We now ask a different question:

Given a crystal structure, how can we calculate its energy?

This sounds like a request for a single number. It is not. A first-principles calculation contains both a physical model and a set of numerical approximations. Understanding the difference between them is one of the most important habits in computational mineralogy.

We will again use MgO. This is deliberate. Periclase is structurally simple, small enough that calculations are fast, and still a genuine periodic ionic solid. We can therefore concentrate on the logic of the calculation rather than on the complexity of the mineral.

A map of the calculation

The calculation can be viewed as a sequence:

crystal structure
      ↓
electronic-structure model
      ├── basis set
      └── exchange-correlation functional
      ↓
numerical choices
      ├── reciprocal-space sampling
      ├── integral thresholds
      └── SCF convergence threshold
      ↓
self-consistent solution
      ↓
total energy
      ↓
numerical convergence test

The distinction between the two middle levels is essential.

Model accuracy asks whether the chosen physical approximation is suitable for the scientific problem. A different exchange-correlation functional, for example, defines a different approximation to the electronic problem.

Numerical convergence asks whether the chosen model has been solved accurately enough. Increasing the number of k-points does not change PBE into a better density functional; it reduces one numerical error within a PBE calculation.

A calculation can therefore be tightly converged and still disagree with experiment because the physical model is incomplete. Conversely, a sophisticated physical model can give poor results if its numerical parameters are not converged.

Density functional theory, without the machinery

The electrons in a mineral require a quantum-mechanical description. Solving the full interacting many-electron problem directly is impractical for the systems of interest here. Density functional theory (DFT) reformulates the problem around the electron density and leads to a practical set of Kohn–Sham equations that are solved iteratively.

The part of the energy associated with exchange and correlation is not known exactly for general systems and must be approximated by an exchange-correlation functional.

A useful qualitative organization is often called Jacob’s ladder:

Level Typical family General idea
1 LDA depends on the local density
2 GGA adds information on density gradients
3 meta-GGA includes additional semilocal information
4 hybrid mixes part of exact Hartree–Fock exchange

Going upward generally changes both the mathematical model and the computational cost. It does not mean that a higher rung is automatically better for every material and every property.

In this laboratory the functional is PBE, a GGA functional. The functional is fixed: it is not part of today’s convergence experiment.

The basis set: the language used for the orbitals

CRYSTAL represents crystalline orbitals using localized Gaussian basis functions centred on atoms. A basis set is therefore the numerical language used to represent the electronic wavefunctions.

A larger and more flexible basis can represent the electronic structure more accurately, but generally increases computational cost and may introduce its own numerical difficulties.

For this laboratory we use the internally available POB-TZVP-REV2 basis set. It is already selected for you. You should be able to recognize the basis-set section of the input, but you are not being asked to optimize the basis set.

NoteFixed does not mean universally optimal

PBE and POB-TZVP-REV2 are kept fixed so that we can isolate one numerical question. Their use here does not establish that this combination is the best possible model for every MgO property.

The self-consistent field cycle

The Kohn–Sham equations depend on the electron density, but the density is also obtained from their solution. CRYSTAL therefore solves the problem self-consistently:

  1. start from an initial electron density;
  2. construct the Kohn–Sham matrix;
  3. solve for the electronic states;
  4. build a new density;
  5. compare the new solution with the previous one;
  6. repeat until the chosen convergence criterion is satisfied.

This is the SCF cycle.

CRYSTAL prints the total energy during the iterations. A successful calculation eventually contains a line of the form:

SCF ENDED - CONVERGENCE ON ENERGY

The analysis tools used in this laboratory deliberately require this convergence marker. The last printed energy of an interrupted or unconverged calculation is not accepted as a valid result.

Why a periodic solid needs k-points

The electronic states of a periodic crystal are described in reciprocal space. In principle, quantities involving the electronic structure require integration over the Brillouin zone. Numerically, this integral is replaced by a finite set of k-points.

CRYSTAL uses the SHRINK keyword to construct a commensurate Monkhorst–Pack-type grid. For the cubic MgO calculation used here, a value \(IS\) corresponds to a nominal sampling based on reciprocal-lattice increments

\[ \frac{\mathbf{b}_1}{IS},\qquad \frac{\mathbf{b}_2}{IS},\qquad \frac{\mathbf{b}_3}{IS}. \]

It is therefore convenient to think of the sequence as increasingly dense nominal \(IS\times IS\times IS\) grids.

This does not mean that CRYSTAL diagonalizes the Kohn–Sham matrix at \(IS^3\) independent points. Crystal symmetry reduces the sampling to the irreducible Brillouin zone (IBZ), and the output reports the actual number of irreducible k-points used.

Increasing SHRINK therefore changes two things that we can observe directly:

  • the numerical approximation to Brillouin-zone integration;
  • the computational work required by the calculation.

Our computational experiment

A good convergence test behaves like a controlled experiment. We keep everything fixed except the parameter being investigated.

For this laboratory:

Quantity Choice
Crystal MgO, fixed geometry
Space group \(Fm\bar{3}m\)
DFT functional PBE
Basis set POB-TZVP-REV2
Integral thresholds fixed
SCF energy threshold fixed
Hardware request 2 physical cores
MPI/OpenMP layout 1 MPI rank × 2 threads
Variable SHRINK = 2,3,\ldots,9

The first calculation, SHRINK = 2, is performed together. You then copy the input, change only the shrinking factor and submit the remaining calculations.

From an input file to a calculation

A simplified CRYSTAL input can be mentally divided into four parts:

STRUCTURE
    lattice / space group / atomic positions

BASIS SET
    numerical representation of atomic orbitals

HAMILTONIAN
    DFT and exchange-correlation functional

NUMERICAL CONTROL
    integral thresholds
    SCF threshold
    SHRINK

You do not need to memorize CRYSTAL keywords. The important skill is to recognize which scientific or numerical choice each block controls.

The official CRYSTAL documentation is available at:

https://www.crystal.unito.it/documentation.html

The manual is the authoritative reference when you later need a keyword that is not explained in this course.

Running the calculation on CluMiner

CRYSTAL is licensed software and is run on the university cluster, not inside Codespaces.

For this laboratory the standard submission command is:

cluminer-crystal mgo_shrink2.d12 --cores-per-node 2 --threads 2

The same resource request must be used for every calculation in the convergence series. With these settings the launcher uses one MPI rank with two OpenMP threads.

Keeping the hardware request fixed is important: if the computational resources changed from one calculation to another, timing comparisons would be much less informative.

The scientific output is returned with the input basename, for example:

mgo_shrink2.d12  →  mgo_shrink2.out

Copy the resulting .out files back into:

work/lab02/outputs/

Reading the result

We will inspect one output manually before using the parser.

The output reports:

  • the Monkhorst shrinking factors;
  • the number of k-points in the irreducible Brillouin zone;
  • the sequence of SCF cycles;
  • the final converged total energy;
  • elapsed and CPU time;
  • the normal termination of the calculation.

This manual inspection matters. Automated parsing should save us from repetitive transcription, not hide the origin of the data.

Energy convergence

For a sequence ordered by increasing SHRINK, the notebook calculates the change relative to the preceding sampling:

\[ \Delta E_i = E_i-E_{i-1}. \]

Because the sign is not important for the convergence criterion, we will normally inspect

\[ |\Delta E_i|. \]

The same value is expressed in hartree and in kJ mol\(^{-1}\) of MgO.

For this CRYSTAL model the primitive computational cell contains one MgO formula unit, so the conversion can be interpreted directly per mole of MgO:

\[ 1\ E_\mathrm{h} \approx 2625.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}. \]

The notebook calculates the conversion from physical constants rather than asking you to perform it manually.

Convergence is a decision, not a magic number

The densest calculation is not automatically the best practical choice.

As SHRINK increases, the energy should become progressively less sensitive to further refinement, while computational cost generally increases. Your task is to choose a sampling that is sufficiently converged for the purpose of the subsequent calculations without paying for unnecessary numerical precision.

Values such as \(0.1\) or \(0.01\) kJ mol\(^{-1}\) can be useful scales when looking at the table, but they are not universal laws. The necessary precision depends on the scientific question. Resolving a very small phase-energy difference may require a tighter criterion than obtaining a preliminary equilibrium structure.

The final question of the laboratory is therefore not:

What is the largest SHRINK value?

but:

Which SHRINK value would you carry forward, and why is its balance between numerical stability and computational cost adequate?

That decision will matter in the next activity, when the geometry of MgO is allowed to change.
