Laboratory 3 — From equilibrium structure to an equation of state

The structure used in an electronic-structure calculation usually comes from experiment. That is an excellent place to begin, but it is not automatically the equilibrium structure of the computational model.

This distinction is easy to overlook. A CIF describes a structure observed under particular experimental conditions. The static DFT calculation used in this laboratory describes a different idealized situation: the electronic ground-state energy of a periodic crystal within a chosen functional and basis set, without thermal expansion and without vibrational free-energy contributions.

Laboratory 3 therefore begins by asking:

Where is the minimum of the energy surface defined by our DFT model?

Once that minimum has been found, we deliberately move away from it and ask:

How costly is it, in energy, to compress or expand the crystal?

The answer leads directly to an equation of state and to one of the most important mechanical properties of a mineral: the bulk modulus.

Why optimize an experimental structure?

Suppose that an experimental CIF gives a cell parameter \(a_\mathrm{exp}\) and a set of atomic coordinates. If we evaluate the DFT energy at that geometry, we obtain a perfectly valid energy for that particular configuration. But the geometry is not guaranteed to minimize the energy of our computational model.

Several reasons contribute to this.

First, an experimental lattice parameter is normally measured at a finite temperature, often close to ambient conditions. Thermal expansion is therefore already present in the observation. Our present calculation is a static electronic-structure calculation. It does not yet contain the vibrational free energy responsible for finite-temperature expansion, and it does not include zero-point vibrational effects unless they are added explicitly later.

Second, the chosen density functional and basis set define an approximate energy surface. Even at exactly \(0\) K and zero external pressure, their predicted equilibrium geometry need not coincide perfectly with experiment.

The purpose of geometry optimization is therefore not to “correct” the experiment. It is to find the structure that is internally consistent with the computational model we have chosen.

Forces, stress and the minimum-energy structure

For a periodic crystal, geometry may involve both:

  • internal degrees of freedom, describing atomic positions within the cell;
  • cell degrees of freedom, describing lattice parameters and cell shape.

At equilibrium, the gradient of the energy with respect to every allowed degree of freedom should vanish within the selected numerical tolerances.

Atomic forces are related to derivatives of the energy with respect to atomic coordinates:

\[ \mathbf{F}_i = -\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}. \]

The stress tensor plays the corresponding role for deformation of the unit cell. If no external pressure or stress is applied, the equilibrium cell is the one for which the residual internal stress vanishes within the numerical accuracy of the calculation.

This distinction matters particularly for MgO.

Why MgO is an unusually clean example

Periclase has the rocksalt structure, space group \(Fm\bar{3}m\). Mg and O occupy special symmetry positions. Their fractional coordinates are fixed by the space-group symmetry.

This has an interesting consequence: the atomic forces may already vanish by symmetry even when the lattice parameter is not the equilibrium one.

The structure can therefore require a geometry optimization without any atomic coordinate moving at all. What changes is the unit cell, driven by its residual stress.

This is a useful reminder that

zero atomic forces

does not necessarily mean

fully optimized periodic crystal.

The cell itself may still be away from equilibrium.

Full geometry optimization in CRYSTAL

CRYSTAL performs geometry optimization through the OPTGEOM block. In this laboratory we use:

OPTGEOM
FULLOPTG
...
END

FULLOPTG allows optimization of both the cell and the internal coordinates that are permitted by symmetry.

The optimization is stopped when numerical criteria on the gradient, displacement and energy change are satisfied.

For the course calculation we use:

TOLDEG = 0.00001
TOLDEX = 0.00004
TOLDEE = 10^-8

The first two are criteria on the RMS gradient and RMS displacement, respectively; TOLDEE controls the energy difference between optimization steps.

These are numerical stopping conditions. They do not define the physical model and they do not make the DFT approximation itself more accurate.

The CRYSTAL23 manual documents FULLOPTG, CVOLOPT, TOLDEG, TOLDEX and TOLDEE in the OPTGEOM input block:

https://www.crystal.unito.it/documentation.html

Which cell is CRYSTAL printing?

MgO provides another useful crystallographic trap.

The familiar crystallographic description is a conventional cubic face-centred cell with one cubic lattice parameter \(a\).

A primitive cell of the same FCC lattice can instead be described by three equal vectors separated by \(60^\circ\). Its numerical cell parameters therefore look very different even though it represents exactly the same infinite crystal.

CRYSTAL may print different cell representations in different parts of an output. When a cell no longer “looks cubic”, do not immediately conclude that the cubic symmetry has been lost. First identify whether you are looking at a primitive or crystallographic representation.

That distinction will return when Quantas normalizes energy–volume data.

From one equilibrium point to a compression curve

Once the static equilibrium geometry is known, we can investigate how the crystal responds to changes in volume.

Experimentally, high-pressure mineral physics often approaches this problem using X-ray diffraction in a diamond-anvil cell. Pressure is applied to the sample and the evolution of its lattice parameters and volume is measured.

A static electronic-structure calculation can approach the same constitutive behaviour from a complementary direction. Instead of physically applying a pressure, we calculate a set of structures at selected volumes and evaluate their energies.

This produces an energy–volume path:

\[ E(V). \]

The pressure associated with that path is obtained from the derivative

\[ P(V) = -\frac{dE}{dV}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ E(V)-E(V_\mathrm{ref}) = -\int_{V_\mathrm{ref}}^{V} P(V')\,dV'. \]

This is why an energy–volume calculation contains the information needed to reconstruct a static pressure–volume relation.

What is an equation of state?

In thermodynamics, an equation of state is a mathematical relation between state variables. For a solid under compression we are especially interested in the relation between pressure and volume.

In the present static calculation, the EOS is parameterized by fitting the computed \(E(V)\) data. The fit provides a smooth representation of the energy surface and therefore, through \(P=-dE/dV\), a smooth pressure–volume relation.

We will use a third-order Birch–Murnaghan equation of state (BM3).

The purpose here is not to derive the finite-strain formalism. The important point is what the fitted parameters mean:

  • \(E_0\): minimum static energy;
  • \(V_0\): equilibrium volume;
  • \(K_0\): bulk modulus at equilibrium;
  • \(K'_0\): first pressure derivative of the bulk modulus.

The fitted \(V_0\) should be close to the volume obtained from the direct geometry optimization. If it is not, that discrepancy is something to investigate.

Bulk modulus: how difficult is the crystal to compress?

The isothermal bulk modulus is defined as

\[ K = -V\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T. \]

At the equilibrium volume,

\[ K_0 = -V_0 \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_{V_0}. \]

A large bulk modulus means that a large pressure change is required to produce a given fractional change in volume: the material is relatively incompressible. A smaller bulk modulus corresponds to a more compressible material.

In the present calculation \(K_0\) is a static bulk modulus derived from the DFT energy surface. Temperature-dependent experimental values need not be identical.

Why constant-volume optimization?

To construct an EOS we need the minimum energy at each selected volume.

If we simply changed the volume and then performed another completely free FULLOPTG calculation, every point would attempt to return to the same equilibrium volume. We would destroy the independent variable of the experiment.

CRYSTAL therefore provides:

CVOLOPT

for full geometry optimization at constant volume.

The logic is:

choose V
   ↓
keep V fixed
   ↓
relax all structural degrees of freedom
that remain allowed
   ↓
obtain the minimum E compatible with V

For a lower-symmetry mineral this may involve genuine changes in cell shape and internal coordinates.

For cubic MgO the situation is particularly simple. At fixed volume the cubic symmetry fixes the cell shape, and the Mg and O coordinates are fixed by their special positions. Almost no structural freedom remains. A constant-volume optimization may therefore converge essentially immediately.

That apparently trivial result is itself instructive:

symmetry determines which structural degrees of freedom exist.

Choosing the volume series

The reference volume is the volume of the optimized static structure, \(V_0\).

We use the factors

\[ \frac{V}{V_0} = 0.91,\; 0.94,\; 0.97,\; 1.00,\; 1.03,\; 1.06,\; 1.09. \]

For a cubic crystal,

\[ V=a^3. \]

Therefore the lattice parameter cannot be scaled by the same numerical factor as the volume. For a target volume factor \(f\),

\[ a(f)=a_0 f^{1/3}. \]

This cube-root relation matters. Setting \(a=0.91a_0\) would not give \(V=0.91V_0\); it would give

\[ V=(0.91)^3V_0 \approx 0.754V_0, \]

which is a much stronger compression.

The notebook calculates the correct target lattice parameters for you.

The role of Quantas

The EOS analysis is performed with Quantas 2.0.

Quantas can read a list of CRYSTAL outputs, normalize their structural and energetic information consistently, generate an energy–volume dataset and fit an integrated EOS. For this laboratory we ask it to express the results in the conventional crystallographic cell, which makes the fitted cubic lattice parameter directly comparable with the MgO cell used in the course.

The Quantas energy–volume workflow reconstructs pressure from the fitted energy relation using

\[ P(V)=-\frac{dE}{dV}. \]

The current MgO energy–volume tutorial in the Quantas documentation uses the same type of seven-point CRYSTAL dataset and a BM3 fit:

https://quantas.readthedocs.io/en/2.0.0-beta/tutorials/eos_ev.html

The software performs the repetitive numerical work. Your task is to decide whether the data and fitted parameters make physical sense.

The complete workflow

The activity follows one continuous chain:

experimental MgO structure
        ↓
full static geometry optimization
        ↓
DFT equilibrium structure
        ↓
choose V/V0 values
        ↓
constant-volume calculations
        ↓
E(V) dataset
        ↓
BM3 fit
        ↓
V0, K0, K'0
        ↓
P(V) and interpretation

This is the first laboratory in which a sequence of electronic-structure calculations becomes a macroscopic mineral property.
