Laboratory 4 — From phonons to thermodynamics

In the previous laboratories we treated a crystal first as a structure, then as an electronic-energy problem, and finally as a mechanically compressible solid. Temperature has so far played almost no role.

This laboratory introduces the missing microscopic ingredient: lattice vibrations.

The objective is not simply to calculate a list of frequencies. We want to follow the complete chain

equilibrium structure
        ↓
small atomic displacements
        ↓
force constants
        ↓
normal modes / phonons
        ↓
sampling of the first Brillouin zone
        ↓
statistical populations
        ↓
Fvib(T), S(T), Uvib(T), CV(T)

and then compare those predictions with experiment.

That comparison is deliberately expected to be imperfect. By the end of the laboratory you should be able to distinguish a discrepancy caused by insufficient q-point sampling from one caused by the harmonic approximation itself.

From the potential-energy surface to normal modes

After Laboratory 3, MgO is at a stationary geometry of the static DFT energy surface. Close to that minimum, the energy can be expanded in the atomic displacements \(\mathbf{u}\):

\[ E(\mathbf{u}) \simeq E_0 + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm T} \mathbf{H} \mathbf{u}. \]

\(\mathbf{H}\) is the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives of the energy with respect to atomic displacements.

Stopping the expansion at second order defines the harmonic approximation. The potential-energy surface is locally replaced by a multidimensional quadratic surface. After mass weighting and diagonalization, the coupled atomic motions become independent normal modes with characteristic frequencies.

For an isolated molecule this would already give a finite set of molecular vibrations. A crystal contains an additional ingredient: translational periodicity.

A phonon has both a branch and a wave vector

A periodic crystal can sustain collective vibrations in which equivalent cells do not all move with the same phase. A phonon mode is therefore labelled not only by a branch index \(j\), but also by a wave vector \(\mathbf{q}\):

\[ \nu_{\mathbf{q}j}. \]

The allowed \(\mathbf{q}\) vectors are described in reciprocal space, and the non-redundant reciprocal-space domain is the first Brillouin zone.

For primitive MgO there are two atoms in the primitive cell, so the phonon spectrum contains

\[ 3N = 6 \]

branches at every \(\mathbf{q}\):

  • three acoustic branches;
  • three optical branches.

The frequencies of these branches vary across the Brillouin zone. This variation is the phonon dispersion.

Why the Γ point is special

The centre of the first Brillouin zone is

\[ \Gamma:\qquad \mathbf{q}=0. \]

At \(\Gamma\), equivalent unit cells oscillate in phase.

A \(\Gamma\)-point phonon calculation is not useless or merely incomplete data. It answers a very important experimental question. In ordinary first-order infrared and Raman spectroscopy, the momentum carried by the photon is tiny compared with the dimensions of a crystal Brillouin zone. Crystal-momentum conservation therefore selects phonons very close to \(\mathbf{q}=0\).

This is why calculated \(\Gamma\)-point optical frequencies can be compared with IR and Raman spectra, subject to the appropriate selection rules.

Not every \(\Gamma\) mode is automatically Raman or IR active. Activity depends on symmetry and on how the vibration couples to the electromagnetic field. Nevertheless, the relevant vibrational frequencies for first-order optical spectroscopy are \(\Gamma\)-point frequencies.

This gives \(\Gamma\) two very different roles in this laboratory:

  • it is physically important for spectroscopy;
  • it is generally insufficient for bulk thermodynamics.

Understanding why those statements are simultaneously true is one of the main objectives of the activity.

Why Γ alone is not enough for thermodynamics

Thermodynamic properties depend on the population of the vibrational states available throughout the crystal.

In the harmonic approximation, a property can be written schematically as a weighted sum over all phonon branches and q-points:

\[ X(T) = \frac{1}{\sum_{\mathbf q}w_{\mathbf q}} \sum_{\mathbf q} w_{\mathbf q} \sum_j X_{\mathbf qj}(T). \]

The precise oscillator expressions are implemented by Quantas. You are not expected to memorize them. The important information flow is

frequency ν(q,j)
      +
q-point integration weight
      +
temperature
      ↓
thermodynamic contribution

The failure of a \(\Gamma\)-only thermodynamic calculation is especially clear for the acoustic branches.

At exactly \(\Gamma\), the three acoustic modes correspond to rigid translations and have frequencies that ideally approach zero. Away from \(\Gamma\) their frequencies increase continuously. Those finite-q acoustic states are a major part of the low-frequency phonon spectrum and dominate the low-temperature heat capacity.

A calculation that contains only \(\Gamma\) therefore misses the dispersion of the acoustic branches almost completely.

This is very different from spectroscopy: the same \(\Gamma\) calculation can contain useful IR/Raman optical frequencies while remaining a very poor sampling of the vibrational states needed for thermodynamics.

Direct-space supercells and reciprocal-space sampling

CRYSTAL uses a direct-space approach to phonon dispersion calculations.

With SCELPHONO, an integer supercell is constructed from the primitive cell. For the isotropic supercells used here,

1×1×1
2×2×2
3×3×3
4×4×4

the corresponding commensurate reciprocal-space meshes contain

direct-space sampling full q-point mesh
\(1\times1\times1\) 1
\(2\times2\times2\) 8
\(3\times3\times3\) 27
\(4\times4\times4\) 64

The relation is reciprocal: increasing the range represented in direct space allows a denser set of exactly commensurate q-points in reciprocal space.

The CRYSTAL phonon-dispersion workflow uses SCELPHONO in the geometry section and DISPERSI inside FREQCALC. The official CRYSTAL tutorial uses exactly this construction for convergence of thermodynamic properties:

https://tutorials.crystalsolutions.eu/tutorial.html?td=dispersion&tf=dispersion-ht

Why CRYSTAL prints 8, 27 or 64 frequency sets

For the calculations used in this laboratory, CRYSTAL explicitly prints a frequency spectrum for every point of the full commensurate q mesh.

Thus a \(4\times4\times4\) calculation contains 64 q-point spectra.

At first sight this looks wasteful because many q-points are related by crystal symmetry. For thermodynamic integration there are two mathematically equivalent ways to represent the sampling.

A full uniform mesh can be written as

\[ \frac{1}{N_q} \sum_{\mathbf q}^{N_q} X(\mathbf q). \]

Alternatively, symmetry-equivalent q-points can be grouped into irreducible representatives:

\[ \frac{1}{\sum_i w_i} \sum_i w_i X(\mathbf q_i), \]

where \(w_i\) is the multiplicity of representative \(\mathbf q_i\).

The second form is what you may have encountered in codes such as Phonopy.

What we do in this course

Quantas can use the full CRYSTAL mesh directly. Equal weights are correct for a complete uniform mesh, and Quantas normalizes all supplied q-point weights by their sum.

For inspection, the course tools can also use spglib to group the full mesh into symmetry-equivalent sets and report an irreducible q-point table with multiplicity weights.

The weights are derived from crystal symmetry, not guessed from similar frequencies. Similar spectra are then used only as a consistency check that symmetry-related q-points really do carry equivalent vibrational information.

This distinction is important. An incorrect q-point weight changes every integrated vibrational property even if the YAML file itself remains perfectly valid.

Quantas deliberately does not invent missing multiplicities from q-point coordinates during an HA/QHA calculation. Weight provenance is part of the scientific input:

https://quantas.readthedocs.io/en/2.0.0-beta/formats/phonon_yaml.html

From phonons to statistical thermodynamics

In statistical thermodynamics, each harmonic phonon behaves as a quantum harmonic oscillator. Once the frequency spectrum is known, temperature determines how strongly each oscillator is populated.

Quantas uses the q-resolved phonon spectrum to calculate quantities including:

  • zero-point vibrational energy;
  • vibrational internal energy;
  • vibrational Helmholtz free energy;
  • vibrational entropy;
  • isochoric heat capacity \(C_V\).

This is the computational version of the connection developed in the statistical-thermodynamics part of the course: the phonon spectrum defines the available vibrational states, while thermal populations determine macroscopic entropy and heat capacity.

The Quantas harmonic workflow is documented here:

https://quantas.readthedocs.io/en/2.0.0-beta/api/ha.html

Heat capacity and q-point convergence

Heat capacity is particularly useful for this laboratory because its physical limits are easy to interpret.

At low temperature, only low-frequency states are populated. The acoustic phonons close to \(\Gamma\) are therefore especially important. A poor q mesh has very few points in this region, and the low-temperature heat capacity is highly sensitive to sampling.

For a three-dimensional crystal with ordinary acoustic dispersion, the low-temperature behaviour approaches the Debye form

\[ C_V \propto T^3. \]

At high temperature, every harmonic vibrational degree of freedom approaches its classical limit. For a crystal with \(n\) atoms per chemical formula unit,

\[ C_V \longrightarrow 3nR. \]

MgO has two atoms per formula unit, so

\[ C_V \longrightarrow 6R \approx 49.9\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}. \]

This is the Dulong–Petit limit for the harmonic heat capacity of MgO.

The progression

Γ → 2×2×2 → 3×3×3 → 4×4×4

should therefore be interpreted as a numerical convergence experiment on the Brillouin-zone integral.

The question is not whether 64 q-points are intrinsically “correct”. The question is whether further refinement changes the observable by an amount that matters for the scientific interpretation.

Comparison with experimental heat capacity

The workspace contains a small experimental reference dataset for MgO.

Low-temperature values come from the measurements of Barron, Berg and Morrison compiled in the NIST Structural Ceramics Database. Higher-temperature values come from an evaluated calorimetric MgO dataset in the same NIST database.

The comparison is intentionally not between identical thermodynamic quantities.

The harmonic calculation provides

\[ C_V, \]

the heat capacity at constant volume.

Experimental calorimetry on a solid under approximately ambient pressure is normally interpreted as a constant-pressure heat capacity,

\[ C_P. \]

At sufficiently low temperature the difference is small. At higher temperature, thermal expansion makes the distinction increasingly important.

This is not an inconvenience to hide. It is part of the laboratory.

The central limitation of the harmonic approximation

In the harmonic approximation used here, the frequencies are calculated at one fixed equilibrium structure. They do not change when temperature changes.

Consequently the harmonic model has no mechanism for thermal expansion:

\[ \alpha_V=0. \]

Within that fixed-volume harmonic description,

\[ C_P=C_V. \]

A real crystal expands with temperature, and its phonon spectrum changes as the volume changes. The real constant-pressure heat capacity therefore contains a contribution that our present HA calculation cannot reproduce.

Even after the \(3\times3\times3\) and \(4\times4\times4\) results become numerically indistinguishable, a discrepancy with experiment may remain.

That residual difference can contain several contributions:

  • the fixed-volume harmonic approximation;
  • the experimental \(C_P\) versus calculated \(C_V\) distinction;
  • the volume dependence of phonon frequencies;
  • explicit anharmonic interactions between phonons;
  • residual DFT/basis-set model error;
  • experimental uncertainty and sample differences.

The first two are particularly important for interpreting this laboratory.

Numerical convergence is not physical exactness

This returns us to a distinction already encountered in Laboratory 2.

A \(4\times4\times4\) phonon calculation may be numerically converged with respect to q-point sampling.

That does not mean the underlying physical model has become exact.

The logic is:

Γ
↓
large q-sampling error

2×2×2
↓
smaller q-sampling error

3×3×3
↓
small q-sampling error

4×4×4
↓
q-point convergence

still different from experiment?
↓
look at the physical approximations

This is the scientific conclusion of the laboratory, not a failure of the calculation.

Looking ahead: beyond the harmonic approximation

If time permits, a later activity can extend the calculation to the quasi-harmonic approximation (QHA).

The QHA repeats harmonic phonon calculations at different volumes. Frequencies then become functions of volume,

\[ \nu_{\mathbf qj}=\nu_{\mathbf qj}(V), \]

allowing the free-energy minimum to move with temperature. Thermal expansion and the distinction between \(C_P\) and \(C_V\) then emerge naturally.

Quantas describes this extension explicitly:

https://quantas.readthedocs.io/en/2.0.0-beta/theory/qha.html

That possible extension is not required for Laboratory 4. Here the goal is first to understand what the harmonic model can predict, how its reciprocal- space sampling converges, and where its physical limits appear in comparison with experiment.
