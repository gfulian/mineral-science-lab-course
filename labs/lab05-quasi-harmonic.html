Laboratory 5 — From harmonic phonons to finite-temperature properties

Laboratory 4 showed that a harmonic phonon calculation can describe important features of a crystal very well. Once the first Brillouin zone is sampled adequately, the vibrational entropy and the constant-volume heat capacity become numerically converged, and the high-temperature harmonic heat capacity approaches the Dulong–Petit limit.

However, it also exposed a limitation that cannot be removed by adding more q-points.

Indeed, in the harmonic approximation used so far, the crystal is represented at one fixed volume. Its phonon frequencies are calculated for that structure and are then used at every temperature. The vibrational populations change with temperature, but the equilibrium structure itself does not.

A real crystal does not behave this way because it normally expands when heated, the phonon frequencies change as interatomic distances change, and thermodynamic properties measured at constant pressure are no longer identical to the corresponding fixed-volume quantities.

The quasi-harmonic approximation (QHA) is one of the simplest and most useful ways to introduce this coupling between vibrations and crystal volume. This approach does not abandon harmonic lattice dynamics; instead, it asks a new question:

How does the harmonic phonon spectrum change when the crystal volume changes?

That additional information is enough to recover thermal expansion and a large set of temperature- and pressure-dependent thermodynamic and thermoelastic properties.

For a mechanically stable and only weakly explicitly anharmonic material such as MgO, this approach is particularly effective.

What was missing from the harmonic calculation?

In the harmonic approximation, the potential-energy surface is replaced locally by a quadratic form around one equilibrium geometry. Each normal mode is then an independent harmonic oscillator with a fixed frequency.

For the calculation performed in Laboratory 4, we may write schematically

\[ \nu_{\mathbf q j} = \nu_{\mathbf q j}(V_0), \]

where \(V_0\) is the selected equilibrium volume.

Temperature changes the occupation of these vibrational states, but it does not change the frequencies themselves. Consequently, the thermodynamic calculation is carried out at one fixed volume. Within this model,

\[ \alpha_V = 0, \]

and therefore

\[ C_P = C_V. \]

This is why the harmonic \(C_V\) can converge perfectly with respect to q-point sampling and still fail to reproduce the high-temperature experimental constant-pressure heat capacity (\(C_P\)).

The numerical problem may be solved while the physical approximation remains.

ImportantNumerical convergence and physical completeness are different questions

Increasing the phonon supercell from \(2\times2\times2\) to \(3\times3\times3\) or \(4\times4\times4\) improves the Brillouin-zone integration.

It does not allow a fixed-volume harmonic crystal to expand with temperature.

Laboratory 5 changes the physical model, not merely the numerical sampling.

The central idea of the quasi-harmonic approximation

The QHA replaces one harmonic calculation by a series of harmonic calculations at different volumes. At each volume the lattice dynamics are still harmonic:

\[ E(\mathbf u;V) \simeq E_0(V) + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf u^{\mathrm T} \mathbf H(V) \mathbf u. \]

However, the Hessian now changes from one volume to another., thus the phonon frequencies become functions of volume:

\[ \nu_{\mathbf qj} = \nu_{\mathbf qj}(V). \]

This is the essential quasi-harmonic step.

The frequencies do not acquire an explicit temperature dependence at fixed volume. Instead, temperature changes the equilibrium volume, and the changing volume changes the frequencies.

The sequence is therefore

temperature
    ↓
equilibrium volume changes
    ↓
phonon frequencies change because ν = ν(V)
    ↓
vibrational free energy changes
    ↓
new equilibrium volume

The equilibrium is obtained self-consistently from the free-energy surface.

QHA contains anharmonic physics — but not explicit anharmonicity

The name quasi-harmonic is important. At every individual volume, the phonons are treated as harmonic oscillators. No cubic or quartic force constants are required for the standard QHA.

Nevertheless, a collection of harmonic calculations at different volumes contains part of the effect that an anharmonic crystal potential has on thermodynamics. The volume dependence of the frequencies allows the crystal to expand and produces a thermal pressure.

This contribution is often called implicit or quasi-harmonic anharmonicity.

The QHA does not include explicit interactions between phonons at a fixed volume. It therefore does not directly describe phenomena such as:

  • phonon lifetimes and linewidths;
  • intrinsic temperature-induced frequency shifts at fixed volume;
  • strong phonon–phonon scattering;
  • strongly anharmonic soft modes;
  • many displacive phase transitions;
  • behaviour close to melting.

For MgO over a moderate pressure–temperature range, explicit anharmonicity is small enough that the QHA provides a very useful description. That is one reason why MgO is an excellent teaching system for this laboratory.

What data are required?

A quasi-harmonic calculation combines two types of information that have already appeared separately in the course.

For a set of volumes \(V_i\), we need the static electronic energy

\[ U_0(V_i) \]

and the harmonic phonon spectrum

\[ \nu_{\mathbf qj}(V_i). \]

The workflow therefore looks like:

choose a set of volumes around equilibrium
            ↓
relax the structure at each fixed volume
            ↓
calculate static energy U0(V)
            ↓
calculate a converged phonon spectrum at each V
            ↓
obtain ν(q,j,V)
            ↓
build F(V,T)
            ↓
minimize at each P,T
            ↓
derive finite-temperature properties

The same electronic-structure model and numerical settings must be used at all volumes, and the phonon sampling must also be consistent.

Laboratory 4 established why the q-point mesh matters. The QHA does not remove that requirement: every volume must contain a phonon description of comparable quality.

In the practical activity, the expensive dense-supercell phonon calculations will be supplied as a precomputed, internally consistent dataset. The purpose of the laboratory is to understand and analyse the quasi-harmonic model rather than to spend the session waiting for multiple large phonon calculations.

From frequencies to vibrational free energy

For a phonon of frequency \(\nu\), the harmonic Helmholtz contribution contains both zero-point and thermal terms. Summed over the Brillouin zone,

\[ F_{\mathrm{vib}}(V,T) = \frac{1}{\sum_{\mathbf q} w_{\mathbf q}} \sum_{\mathbf q,j} w_{\mathbf q} \left[ \frac{1}{2}h\nu_{\mathbf qj}(V) + k_BT \ln \left( 1- e^{-h\nu_{\mathbf qj}(V)/(k_BT)} \right) \right]. \]

The total Helmholtz free energy is

\[ F(V,T) = U_0(V) + F_{\mathrm{vib}}(V,T). \]

This equation is the heart of the QHA.

The first term describes the static electronic energy surface already studied through the equation of state in Laboratory 3.

The second term describes the vibrational free energy studied in Laboratory 4, but now the vibrational spectrum is allowed to change with volume.

The two earlier laboratories therefore meet in a single quantity:

Laboratory 3                         Laboratory 4
static E(V)                          phonons and Fvib(T)
      \                                  /
       \                                /
        └────── F(V,T) = U0(V)+Fvib(V,T)
                          ↓
                         QHA

Zero-point motion already changes the equilibrium

At \(T=0\), the thermal population of excited phonons vanishes, but the zero-point term

\[ \frac{1}{2}h\nu_{\mathbf qj}(V) \]

remains.

Therefore the minimum of

\[ F(V,0) = U_0(V)+E_{\mathrm{ZP}}(V) \]

need not occur at exactly the same volume as the minimum of the static electronic energy \(U_0(V)\).

This distinction is small for many solids, but conceptually important:

The static DFT equilibrium structure and the zero-temperature quasi-harmonic equilibrium structure are not necessarily identical.

Zero-point vibrations can exert a small zero-point pressure.

Equilibrium at a fixed temperature

At zero external pressure, the equilibrium volume at temperature \(T\) is the minimum of the Helmholtz free-energy curve:

\[ \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial V} \right)_T =0. \]

Imagine calculating \(F(V,T)\) at several temperatures.

At low temperature the minimum lies close to the static equilibrium volume. As temperature rises, the vibrational contribution changes the shape and position of the free-energy minimum.

For an ordinary material with positive thermal expansion, the minimum shifts towards larger volume:

low T       → minimum near V0
higher T    → minimum at larger V

The thermal expansion of the crystal is therefore obtained from a sequence of free-energy minima.

Equilibrium at finite pressure

Pressure enters through the \(PV\) work term.

At external hydrostatic pressure \(P\), the quantity to minimize is

\[ G^*(V;P,T) = F(V,T)+PV. \]

The equilibrium condition is

\[ \left( \frac{\partial G^*}{\partial V} \right)_{P,T} =0, \]

which gives

\[ P = - \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial V} \right)_T. \]

This is the finite-temperature extension of the relation encountered in the static equation-of-state laboratory.

The QHA therefore turns the static relation

\[ E(V) \]

into a temperature-dependent family

\[ F(V,T). \]

From this surface we can determine

\[ V(P,T). \]

That one function is the structural backbone of most of the properties studied below.

A useful geometrical picture

It is helpful to imagine \(F(V,T)\) as a surface.

One axis is volume, one is temperature, and the vertical direction is free energy.

A constant-temperature slice through this surface is an \(F(V)\) curve.

At zero pressure, its minimum gives the equilibrium volume.

At non-zero pressure, adding \(PV\) tilts the curve before the minimum is found.

The complete QHA result can therefore be thought of as a map:

(P,T)
  ↓
equilibrium point on F(V,T)+PV
  ↓
V(P,T)
  ↓
thermodynamic and thermoelastic properties

This is why one calculation can later be displayed either as:

  • a property versus temperature at fixed pressure;
  • a property versus pressure at fixed temperature;
  • a two-dimensional pressure–temperature map.

These are different views of the same thermodynamic model.

Thermal expansion

Once \(V(P,T)\) is known, the volumetric thermal-expansion coefficient is

\[ \alpha_V = \frac{1}{V} \left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial T} \right)_P. \]

Its unit is normally

\[ \mathrm{K^{-1}}. \]

A positive \(\alpha_V\) means that the equilibrium volume increases as temperature increases at constant pressure.

The QHA also provides a more revealing thermodynamic relation.

Since

\[ P(V,T) = - \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial V} \right)_T, \]

one obtains

\[ \alpha_V = - \frac{1}{K_T} \frac{\partial^2F} {\partial V\,\partial T}. \]

Using

\[ S = - \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial T} \right)_V, \]

the same relation becomes

\[ \alpha_V = \frac{1}{K_T} \left( \frac{\partial S}{\partial V} \right)_T. \]

This equation gives a useful physical interpretation:

Thermal expansion occurs because vibrational entropy depends on volume.

The entropy calculated in Laboratory 4 is therefore not an isolated thermodynamic quantity. Its volume dependence is directly connected to the thermal expansion of the crystal.

Linear and axial thermal expansion

Experimental crystallography often reports the variation of lattice parameters rather than only the total cell volume.

For a lattice parameter \(a\),

\[ \alpha_a = \frac{1}{a} \left( \frac{\partial a}{\partial T} \right)_P. \]

For a cubic crystal such as MgO,

\[ V=a^3, \]

so

\[ \alpha_V=3\alpha_a \]

and therefore

\[ \alpha_a=\frac{\alpha_V}{3}. \]

This makes MgO particularly simple.

For anisotropic crystals the situation contains more information.

For an orthorhombic crystal,

\[ \alpha_V = \alpha_a+\alpha_b+\alpha_c. \]

For a hexagonal or trigonal crystal with the conventional hexagonal setting,

\[ \alpha_V = 2\alpha_a+\alpha_c. \]

There is no requirement that \(\alpha_a\) and \(\alpha_c\) be equal. One direction may expand much more strongly than another, and in some materials one direction may even contract while the total volume still expands.

For monoclinic and triclinic crystals, changes in the cell angles also matter. A full description is therefore more naturally expressed through the thermal-expansion tensor rather than through three independent scalar coefficients.

NoteWhat a volume-based QHA structural path actually means

If the crystal structure is relaxed at several constrained volumes, each calculation provides the lowest-energy cell shape and internal coordinates compatible with that volume and symmetry.

A QHA code can interpolate this structural path and evaluate the cell at the equilibrium volume \(V(P,T)\).

For an anisotropic crystal this is extremely useful, but it is still a one-dimensional path through strain space. It is not identical to independently minimizing every strain component at every pressure and temperature.

The isothermal bulk modulus at finite temperature

Laboratory 3 obtained the bulk modulus from the curvature of the static energy–volume relation.

The same idea applies to the free energy.

The isothermal bulk modulus is

\[ K_T(V,T) = V \left( \frac{\partial^2F}{\partial V^2} \right)_T. \]

At the equilibrium volume,

\[ K_T=K_T(P,T). \]

The crystal can therefore become more or less compressible as both pressure and temperature change.

For many minerals, increasing pressure increases the bulk modulus, while increasing temperature at constant pressure tends to reduce it. The detailed response is material dependent and must be obtained from the free-energy surface rather than assumed.

The pressure derivative is

\[ K'_T = \left( \frac{\partial K_T}{\partial P} \right)_T. \]

This is the finite-temperature analogue of the \(K'_0\) parameter encountered in the static equation-of-state analysis.

Two ways to represent the free-energy curve

To find equilibrium properties, the calculated values of \(F(V,T)\) must be represented by a smooth function of volume.

There is no unique numerical representation.

Two useful strategies are a polynomial representation and an equation-of-state representation.

Polynomial representation

At each temperature, the free-energy points can be fitted locally by a polynomial in volume.

The equilibrium condition is then obtained from

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial V}+P=0. \]

Once the polynomial is known, its derivatives provide:

\[ P, \qquad K_T, \qquad K'_T. \]

The principal advantage is flexibility. A low-order polynomial can represent a smooth local free-energy basin without assuming a specific finite-strain EOS.

The main caution is that derivatives amplify imperfections in a fit. This is especially important for higher derivatives such as \(K'_T\). Increasing polynomial degree can reduce residuals while simultaneously making the extrapolation or derivatives less stable.

A polynomial should therefore be treated as a local numerical model, not as a law of nature.

Equation-of-state representation

Alternatively, the free-energy points can be fitted with an integrated equation-of-state form, such as a Birch–Murnaghan model.

The fitted free energy and pressure are constrained by

\[ P(V,T) = - \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial V} \right)_T. \]

The EOS provides a physically motivated finite-strain description of the curvature and compression response.

The bulk modulus then emerges naturally from the EOS parameters and derivatives.

The advantage is a stronger physical structure. The disadvantage is that the chosen EOS family may be too restrictive for a particular interval or material.

Which one is correct?

Neither representation is automatically superior in every calculation. The appropriate question is:

Do reasonable representations of the same free-energy data give stable physical conclusions?

A useful QHA analysis therefore compares polynomial and EOS-based minimization.

If both give similar equilibrium volumes, heat capacities, expansion coefficients and bulk moduli within the well-sampled region, confidence in the result increases.

If they disagree substantially, the problem may lie in:

  • insufficient volume sampling;
  • an unsuitable fitting interval;
  • an inappropriate polynomial degree;
  • an EOS form that is too restrictive;
  • extrapolation outside the sampled volume range;
  • noisy or inconsistent input calculations.

The smallest fit residual is not, by itself, enough to decide which physical result is most trustworthy.

Why the sampled volume range matters

A fit can look numerically excellent and still produce an unreliable QHA result if the equilibrium minimum moves outside the range of calculated volumes.

For every requested \((P,T)\) state, the predicted equilibrium volume should be checked against the sampled interval.

Ideally,

smallest calculated V
        <
equilibrium V(P,T)
        <
largest calculated V

with reasonable margin on both sides.

The farther the result relies on extrapolation, the less the QHA is constrained by actual first-principles data.

This is particularly important at:

  • high temperature and low pressure, where thermal expansion moves the minimum towards larger volumes;
  • high pressure, where compression moves the minimum towards smaller volumes.

A pressure–temperature map is scientifically meaningful only over the region for which the underlying volume data provide adequate coverage.

Grüneisen parameters: how strongly does a phonon respond to volume?

The QHA can also be viewed mode by mode.

For a phonon branch \(j\) at q-point \(\mathbf q\), the mode Grüneisen parameter is

\[ \gamma_{\mathbf qj} = - \frac{\partial\ln\nu_{\mathbf qj}} {\partial\ln V}. \]

If

\[ \gamma_{\mathbf qj}>0, \]

the mode normally softens when the crystal expands.

If

\[ \gamma_{\mathbf qj}<0, \]

the mode stiffens on expansion.

A heat-capacity-weighted average Grüneisen parameter connects microscopic phonons with macroscopic thermal expansion.

In a thermodynamically consistent QHA,

\[ \alpha_V = \frac{\bar{\gamma}C_V}{K_TV}. \]

This compact relation is worth interpreting qualitatively.

Thermal expansion is favoured by:

  • phonons that respond strongly to volume;
  • a large vibrational heat capacity;
  • a relatively compressible crystal.

It is opposed by a large bulk modulus.

The same relation explains why a very stiff material can have modest thermal expansion even when its phonons have appreciable volume dependence.

From constant-volume to constant-pressure heat capacity

This is the connection to the discrepancy observed in Laboratory 4.

Within QHA,

\[ C_P-C_V = \alpha_V^2K_TVT. \]

Therefore

\[ C_P = C_V + \alpha_V^2K_TVT. \]

The correction vanishes in the strictly harmonic fixed-volume model because

\[ \alpha_V=0. \]

Once thermal expansion is allowed, the two heat capacities separate.

This is why QHA can reproduce the experimentally observed increase of constant-pressure heat capacity beyond the harmonic Dulong–Petit value even though the underlying phonons are still harmonic at each fixed volume.

The extra contribution does not represent additional harmonic degrees of freedom. It comes from the coupling between heating and expansion.

Isothermal and adiabatic bulk moduli

Experiments do not always probe the same thermodynamic constraint.

Slow compression with sufficient thermal equilibration is associated with an isothermal bulk modulus \(K_T\).

A sufficiently rapid elastic disturbance is closer to adiabatic conditions and is described by \(K_S\).

Thermodynamics connects them through

\[ K_S = K_T \frac{C_P}{C_V}. \]

Because

\[ C_P\geq C_V, \]

one normally has

\[ K_S\geq K_T. \]

At very low temperature, or whenever the thermal correction is negligible, the two become nearly identical.

This distinction later becomes important when comparing static or thermodynamic calculations with ultrasonic and seismic measurements.

Entropy and Gibbs free energy

At the equilibrium volume, QHA also provides entropy and free energies as functions of pressure and temperature.

The entropy follows from

\[ S = - \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial T} \right)_V, \]

with the final value evaluated consistently at the equilibrium state.

At finite pressure,

\[ G(P,T) = F(P,T)+PV. \]

The Gibbs free energy is especially important because it is the thermodynamic potential used to compare phase stability at fixed pressure and temperature.

For Laboratory 5 we will focus on one stable MgO phase, so phase boundaries are not the objective. Nevertheless, the same framework is the basis for comparing different polymorphs when their free energies are calculated consistently.

What can be obtained from one QHA model?

Once the free-energy surface is available and the equilibrium state has been found, the same calculation can provide a remarkably broad set of properties:

Property Meaning
\(V(P,T)\) equilibrium volume
\(a,b,c,\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) equilibrium cell parameters, when a structural path is available
\(\alpha_V\) volumetric thermal expansion
\(\alpha_a,\alpha_b,\alpha_c\) axial expansion coefficients where meaningful
\(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) thermal-expansion tensor
\(S(P,T)\) entropy
\(C_V(P,T)\) isochoric heat capacity
\(C_P(P,T)\) isobaric heat capacity
\(K_T(P,T)\) isothermal bulk modulus
\(K'_T(P,T)\) pressure derivative of \(K_T\)
\(K_S(P,T)\) adiabatic bulk modulus
\(\gamma(P,T)\) macroscopic Grüneisen parameter
\(\gamma_{\mathbf qj}\) mode Grüneisen parameters when mode continuity is available
\(F,H,G\) thermodynamic potentials

The important lesson is not the length of this list.

It is that these properties are not independent calculations. They are different derivatives or evaluations of the same underlying quasi-harmonic free-energy model.

One-dimensional sections and two-dimensional maps

A pressure–temperature calculation naturally produces a grid.

For example,

\[ X=X(P,T) \]

may represent volume, thermal expansion, bulk modulus or heat capacity.

There are two complementary ways to inspect such a result.

A section at constant pressure

At ambient pressure,

\[ X(T)\big|_{P=0} \]

can be compared directly with many ordinary experimental datasets.

This is particularly useful for:

  • lattice parameters;
  • volume;
  • thermal expansion;
  • entropy;
  • \(C_P\);
  • bulk modulus.

This representation is usually the easiest one to interpret first.

A section at constant temperature

A pressure section,

\[ X(P)\big|_{T=\mathrm{const}}, \]

shows how compression changes the property at a chosen temperature.

This provides a natural bridge to high-pressure experiments.

A two-dimensional \(P\)–\(T\) map

The complete grid can be displayed as a contour or colour map.

For example,

\[ \alpha_V(P,T) \]

may show simultaneously that thermal expansion increases with temperature and is suppressed by pressure.

A two-dimensional map contains more information, but it should normally be interpreted after examining simpler one-dimensional sections.

Otherwise, a visually impressive plot can hide the physical relationships that generated it.

What should be compared with experiment?

The QHA becomes scientifically useful when its predictions are confronted with independent observations.

For MgO, useful experimental comparisons include:

  • lattice parameter or molar/cell volume as a function of temperature;
  • volumetric or linear thermal expansion;
  • entropy;
  • constant-pressure heat capacity;
  • isothermal or adiabatic bulk modulus;
  • pressure–volume data at different temperatures.

Different experimental techniques probe different thermodynamic conditions. The comparison must therefore use the quantity that corresponds to the measurement.

In particular:

harmonic laboratory     → Cv at fixed V
quasi-harmonic model    → Cv and Cp
ordinary calorimetry    → approximately Cp

Similarly, a bulk modulus inferred from a static equation of state is not automatically the same quantity as an adiabatic modulus measured from sound velocities.

Labels and thermodynamic conditions matter.

A hierarchy of approximations

It is useful to place the methods used in the course in a hierarchy.

Static electronic structure

electrons
+
fixed nuclei

gives the static energy surface \(U_0(V)\).

Harmonic approximation

one equilibrium structure
+
quadratic lattice vibrations

adds zero-point and thermal vibrational properties at fixed volume.

Quasi-harmonic approximation

harmonic phonons
at several volumes

adds the leading coupling between vibrations and volume.

Explicit anharmonic methods

These describe temperature-dependent interactions between phonons even at fixed volume and require more advanced approaches, for example higher-order force constants, molecular dynamics, self-consistent phonons or related methods.

The QHA is therefore not the end of lattice thermodynamics. It is the next physically meaningful level after HA, and for many stable minerals it is an extremely useful one.

When should QHA work well?

QHA is most reliable when:

  • the crystal remains mechanically and dynamically stable;
  • the phonon spectrum changes smoothly with volume;
  • explicit anharmonicity is moderate;
  • the temperature remains sufficiently below strongly anharmonic or melting regimes;
  • no soft-mode structural transition is crossed;
  • the sampled volume interval contains the relevant equilibrium states.

It can become unreliable when:

  • imaginary phonons appear in the physically relevant volume range;
  • phonon branches become difficult to track;
  • a phase transition changes the crystal structure;
  • explicit phonon–phonon interactions dominate;
  • equilibrium volumes require substantial extrapolation;
  • electronic, magnetic, configurational or defect contributions become thermodynamically important.

The correct question is therefore not simply:

“Did the QHA calculation finish?”

but:

“Is the QHA physically appropriate and numerically supported over the pressure–temperature region that I want to interpret?”

A note on mode continuity

If individual phonon frequencies are fitted as functions of volume, a mode at one volume must be matched with the corresponding physical mode at the other volumes.

This is called mode continuity.

A naive sorting of frequencies from low to high is not always sufficient, because branches can cross or become degenerate.

For simple, well-behaved systems the correspondence may be straightforward. For more complicated crystals, eigenvector information or a dedicated tracking procedure is preferable.

An alternative QHA strategy is to interpolate already integrated harmonic thermodynamic functions rather than individual phonon branches. This avoids mode-by-mode correspondence, although it no longer provides the same mode-resolved information.

Both approaches are scientifically legitimate when their assumptions are satisfied.

What Quantas does

Quantas implements the QHA as a multi-volume thermodynamic workflow.

It can represent the volume dependence either through mode-resolved frequencies or through integrated harmonic thermodynamic functions. It can minimize the free-energy curves using either polynomial representations or integrated equations of state.

The workflow can then evaluate equilibrium properties on a pressure–temperature grid, including:

  • \(V(P,T)\);
  • \(K_T\) and \(K_S\);
  • \(K'_T\);
  • \(\alpha_V\);
  • axial expansion where structural information is available;
  • \(C_V\) and \(C_P\);
  • entropy;
  • Grüneisen parameters;
  • equilibrium lattice parameters.

For this course, Quantas is the numerical tool. The objective is not to learn its command-line syntax by memory. The objective is to understand the thermodynamic model that the software is evaluating.
